Instructor notes

About 20 minutes, before the first simulator session of a cycle. Controls: arrow keys or page keys to step, N notes (keep notes off the projector), F fullscreen, A reveal everything on a slide, T light or dark.

Message for the whole briefing: the simulator is not a test of whether you make errors. It is a place to practise seeing threats, catching errors and managing them, and the data from this session helps decide what the next pilot trains.

Part 1 · Why we changed

Why we train a new way

BEFORE · TASK-BASED

Did you pass each maneuver?

  • A list of maneuvers, each checked to a standard
  • The same exercises, cycle after cycle
  • Strong on skill. Weak on how the crew handles the unexpected
→
WHAT WE LEARNED

Why it had to change

  • Aircraft are very reliable, so most remaining events come down to how the crew manages them
  • Surprise, automation and communication cannot be trained by repeating maneuvers
  • A pass or fail does not show how the result was reached
→
NOW · CBTA

Can you manage what happens?

  • Nine competencies, seen as behaviours we can observe
  • Scenarios built from real threats and data
  • Debrief on what was seen, not only what was missed
We do not train to pass a check. We train pilots to anticipate threats, catch errors and manage them, so an error never becomes an unsafe outcome.

Instructor notes

Start here, before any questions. The pilots should hear why the training looks different from the maneuver checks they know. Walk the three boxes left to right.

Left: task-based training asked whether each maneuver met a standard. It does that job well. Middle: the reason for change. These are general industry reasons (reliable aircraft, surprise and automation, results without the story behind them) and are DERIVED for this briefing; confirm the wording against ICAO Doc 9868 and Doc 9995 before quoting them. VERIFY. Right: CBTA keeps the maneuvers and adds how the crew got the result.

What NOK states in the manual: the FTM §3.1.10.1 says the Competency Based Assessment methodology will be applied when TCAR PEL Part FCL enters into force, using Part-FCL Appendix 9 (LPC/OPC). CONFIRMED. Check which method applies to a given session today (FTM §3.1.10.1, §3.1.11).

The philosophy line is instructor wording for this briefing, not a manual quote. Ask: which part of your last check was about how you handled something, not whether you passed it?

Q2. A threat comes from outside the crew's control and adds complexity (weather, a late runway change, an MEL item, time pressure). Q3. A sudden unexpected event triggers a body and mind reaction: attention narrows, you may freeze or rush. Good answers: fly the aircraft first, take a moment to regain composure, name what is happening, then act (Fly, Focus, Act).

Part 4 · Threat and error management

Anticipate, Monitor, Respond

THREAT

An event or condition outside the crew's control that adds complexity or reduces safety margins.

Weather, a late runway change, an MEL item, terrain, time pressure.

ERROR

A crew action or inaction that leads to a deviation from the crew's or the company's intentions.

UNDESIRED AIRCRAFT STATE (UAS)

An aircraft position, speed, attitude or configuration, caused by a crew action or inaction, that reduces the margin of safety.

Errors are expected. The aim is to catch and manage them early.

Instructor notes

Read it left to right. Anticipate: see the threat coming and act on it. If the actions work, the outcome is inconsequential. Monitor: if the threat is not managed, or the actions fail, an error follows; keep watching so the error is caught. Respond: actions to manage the error. If they work, the outcome is inconsequential; if they fail, the aircraft is in an undesired state and the crew must recover.

The three phases line up with NOK's own OM-A wording: Avoid (good briefings, early identification of threats), Trap (effective monitoring and following SOPs) and Mitigate (reduce the consequences of errors to acceptable levels), OM-A Chapter 8 p.8-274 and 8-276. CONFIRMED for the OM-A words; the matching of phase to word is DERIVED.

Definitions paraphrase the ICAO threat and error management model (Doc 9803, Doc 9995). VERIFY exact wording before quoting.

Part 5 · When the unexpected happens

Deciding under pressure: ADM and FORDEC

ADM is the habit of deciding with a plan, not by reflex. FORDEC is one simple model for it.

F
Facts

What is happening? What do I know? What do I not know?

EXAMPLE Weather below minimums. Fuel is limited. The alternate is clear.

O
Options

What can I do? Name at least two.

EXAMPLE Hold longer · try one approach · divert now

R
Risks and benefits

What could go wrong with each option?

EXAMPLE Hold: fuel shrinks. Approach: may fail. Divert: certain, costs time.

D
Decision

Choose one. Say it aloud.

EXAMPLE "We divert now."

E
Execution

Who does what, and when?

EXAMPLE Tell ATC. Set the FMC. Brief the cabin.

C
Check

Is it working? New facts?

EXAMPLE Fuel, time, alternate weather. Still right?

Not working? Back to Facts

Illustrative scenario, made up. Non-normal checklist: memory items first, then decide.

Instructor notes

Six steps, one at a time. The EXAMPLE line under each step follows one made-up scenario: holding at the destination while the weather falls below minimums. It is illustrative, not NOK data.

ADM (aeronautical decision making) and FORDEC (Facts, Options, Risks and benefits, Decision, Execution, Check) are used in NOK training (instructor confirmed). The terms are not defined in the NOK manuals I searched (OM-A, FTM, SOP, SMM, handbooks, FCTM), so the step wording on this slide is general industry wording and the example is made up.

When to use it: when there is time. In an emergency, do memory items first as written in the QRH, then decide. The Check step loops back: if it is not working, you have new facts and start again.

Link to the startle slide: FORDEC is what you do after Fly, Focus, Act, once the aircraft is under control. Ask: what is the first fact you would look for in this scenario?

Part 6 · How the system fits together

Two loops: operation and training

Instructor notes

Outer loop = operation (top). Inner loop = training (bottom). Reveal them one at a time. The outer loop runs on the line: flights produce data, the safety system assesses the risk, chooses mitigations, then tracks whether the risk fell. The inner loop runs in the simulator: scenarios are built from what the outer loop found, you fly the session, the instructor assesses and debriefs, and the results go back.

Two links join the loops. Link 1: when training is chosen as a mitigation, it decides which threats and scenarios the next cycle uses. Link 2: training results go back to the safety team so they can see whether the risk fell. Today you are at box 6.

Source for the terms: ICAO Doc 9995 defines the inner loop as the data collected and analysed within the operator's training department, and the outer loop as the data collected and analysed within the operator's safety management system and flight operations department (quoted in the IATA EBT Data Report, 2021 amendment, section 2.6, pp.10-11). CONFIRMED. Note: since 2018 IATA also uses the words for inside versus outside the organization; this slide uses the ICAO Doc 9995 meaning. NOK sources: SMM §6.5 (risk matrix) and §7.10.1 (FDAP risk analysis, mitigation including training, tracking, effectiveness review), p.7-23. CONFIRMED.

Ask: where do your own reports and debrief comments enter this picture? (Box 1 and box 7.)

Instructor notes

The Boeing tools from the previous slides sit in these moments: TEM card before and after, Fly-Focus-Act when something goes wrong, and a clear question when the other pilot needs to be challenged.

Four moments: before (brief the threats), during (scan for threats and errors, yours and the other pilot's), error (fly the aircraft first, then say it and fix it), after (debrief). Tell the pilots plainly: an error you catch and manage is good TEM; an error you hide or ignore is the one that costs you.

The example calls are plain-language illustrations of the behaviour (naming a threat, owning an error, challenging). They are not company standard callouts; use the SOP callouts where one exists.

What you do in the session
