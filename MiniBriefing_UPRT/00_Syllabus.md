## Slide 7 — UPRT Objective & Regulatory Basis

- Objective: support flight crew in acquiring the knowledge and skills required to
  prevent and recover from upsets or loss of control
- Course comprises: UPRT + Flight Path Management (manual/automatic) during
  Unreliable Airspeed Indication and other failures
- Regulatory basis: AMC2 ORO.FC.220&230, AMC1 ORO.FC.120&130 — theoretical + practical
  (FSTD) training, referenced against the AMC1 ORO.FC.120&130 table and Tables 1/2 of
  AMC1 ORO.FC.220&230
- [FTM §2.1.13.1–2.1.13.2, p.2-76]

## Slide 8 — Ground Lessons Overview

Ground-training topic groups per FTM Table 1 (elements of upset prevention training):
- A. Aerodynamics — general characteristics, certification/limitations, high/low
  altitude aerodynamics, performance, AOA & stall awareness, stick shaker/pusher, Mach
  effects, aeroplane stability, control surface fundamentals, use of trims, icing effects
- B. Causes of and contributing factors to upset — environmental, pilot-induced, mechanical
- C. Safety review of accidents/incidents relating to aeroplane upsets
- D. g-load awareness and management — positive/negative/increasing/decreasing g,
  lateral g (sideslip), g-load management
- E. Energy Management — kinetic vs. potential vs. chemical energy (power)
- F. Flight Path Management — pitch/power/performance relationship, manual & automation
  inputs, go-around management, automation management, proper use of rudder
- G. Recognition — physiological/visual/instrument cues, pitch/power/roll/yaw,
  effective scanning, stall protection systems, criteria for identifying stalls/upsets
- H. System malfunction — flight control defects, engine failure, instrument failures,
  loss of reliable airspeed, automation failures, fly-by-wire protection degradation
- [FTM §2.1.13.2, Table 1, p.2-77–2-78]

## Slide 9 — Aerodynamics Behind UPRT: AOA & Stability Fundamentals

- Framing line: "UPRT is not about correcting attitude. It is about restoring
  aerodynamic stability."
- What is stability and control — example disturbances: gust, turbulence, control
  input release, engine thrust change
- Angle of Attack (AOA) is the core of UPRT — **AOA ≠ pitch attitude**
- Critical Angle of Attack: "the critical angle of attack, where the airflow separates
  from the wing causing lift to be degraded, can be exceeded in any attitude, and even
  at different airspeeds if there is more or less than 1G on the airplane"
- Key point: nose-low ≠ low AOA
- [Nok Air UPRT Aerodynamic & Case Study deck, slides 14–25]

## Slide 10 — Aerodynamics Behind UPRT: Load Factor & V-n Diagram

- Load factor (n) = ratio of aircraft lift to its weight
- Relationship: n↑ → CL↑ → α↑ → reaches CLmax → flow separation → control loss
- V-n diagram dynamics in an upset: pilot pulls → load factor increases → aircraft
  moves upward on the V-n diagram; if speed is low, hits stall boundary quickly; if
  speed is high, may exceed structural limit
- [Nok Air UPRT Aerodynamic & Case Study deck, slides 27–28]

## Slide 11 — Aerodynamics Behind UPRT: High Altitude Stability (Coffin Corner)

- At altitude: static margin is smaller, CL-α is reduced, damping is weaker, control
  authority is reduced
- An aggressive pull at altitude increases n and eliminates lift margin quickly
- Implication for recovery: must be smooth, gentle, and accept altitude loss — the
  stability margin is thin
- [Nok Air UPRT Aerodynamic & Case Study deck, slides 30, 42]

## Slide 12 — Aerodynamics Behind UPRT: Stability Breakdown in Upset

- During a nose-high upset: AOA increases, CL approaches CLmax, the lift curve becomes
  nonlinear, the pitching moment curve flattens, stability margin decreases, tail
  effectiveness reduces
- Covers Pitch / Roll / Yaw breakdown individually
- An upset (stability perspective) occurs when: aircraft is outside the normal flight
  envelope, stability margin is reduced, control authority is degraded, aerodynamic
  forces are nonlinear — so recovery is not just "attitude correction"
- Historical upset definition (QRH): pitch >25° nose up, pitch >10° nose down, bank
  >45°, or an airspeed inappropriate for the conditions given less-than-above
  parameters. Current QRH framing: an upset is any time the airplane is diverting from
  the intended airplane state (pitch/roll deviation or inappropriate airspeed)
- [Nok Air UPRT deck slides 32–37 | QRH MAN.1.8 Upset Recovery]

## Slide 13 — The Stability-Based Recovery Model

- Phase 1 — Restore Stability: reduce AOA, restore linear lift region
- Phase 2 — Restore Control Authority: flow reattachment, re-establish roll control
- Phase 3 — Re-align Flight Path: roll wings level, adjust pitch
- Phase 4 — Rebuild Energy: power as required
- Supporting logic:
  - Why unload first — at high AOA, aileron effectiveness is reduced and the tip may be
    stalled; a roll input can deepen the stall asymmetrically. Once AOA is reduced,
    flow reattaches, roll control is restored, lateral stability improves — roll comes
    second
  - Thrust management — power changes modify pitching moment, energy state, and
    climb/descent path, but thrust does NOT directly fix stability; stability must be
    restored first
  - Nose-low recovery (stability view) — stability still exists, but load factor is
    increasing and structure is at risk; unloading reduces n, moves the aircraft
    downward on the V-n diagram, reduces structural stress, and preserves control
    margin — unload first, same as nose-high
- [Nok Air UPRT deck slides 36, 38–43]

## Slide 14 — QRH Procedure: Nose High Recovery / Nose Low Recovery

**Nose High Recovery** — PF: recognize and confirm; disengage autopilot; disengage
autothrottle; recover — apply nose-down elevator (as much as needed for nose-down
pitch rate), apply appropriate nose-down stabilizer trim*, reduce thrust, roll (adjust
bank angle) to obtain a nose-down pitch rate*; complete the recovery — when approaching
the horizon roll to wings level, check airspeed and adjust thrust, establish pitch
attitude. PM: call out attitude/airspeed/altitude throughout, verify actions done, call
out continued deviation.

**Nose Low Recovery** — PF: recognize and confirm; disengage autopilot; disengage
autothrottle; recover — recover from stall if needed, roll in the shortest direction to
wings level (if bank >90°, unload and roll)*; complete the recovery — apply nose-up
elevator, apply nose-up trim if needed*, adjust thrust and drag if needed. PM: same
callout duties as above.

WARNING (both procedures): excessive use of pitch trim or rudder can aggravate an
upset, result in loss of control, or result in high structural loads.

This is the authoritative PF/PM non-normal maneuver checklist — use this wording over
paraphrased technique language when briefing the actual procedure.

- [QRH MAN.1.9–1.10]

## Slide 15 — Case Study: West Caribbean Airways Flight 708 (LOC-I)

- Facts: chartered passenger flight, McDonnell Douglas MD-82, crashed near Machiques,
  Zulia state, Venezuela, 16 August 2005, en route Tocumen Intl (Panama City) →
  Martinique Aimé Césaire Intl, 160 fatalities, entered a deep aerodynamic stall at high
  altitude and never recovered
- Accident sequence: climbed to FL330 with anti-ice systems on — too high for the
  aircraft's weight and atmospheric conditions, leaving insufficient stall margin;
  autopilot tried to hold altitude as airspeed decayed, quietly pitching the nose up
  until the aircraft stalled at high altitude; crew apparently misdiagnosed the
  situation as a dual engine failure and increased pitch instead of lowering the nose
  decisively
- Investigation findings: JIAAC (Venezuela), assisted by BEA (France) and NTSB (US),
  concluded primary cause was pilot error — failure to recognize/recover from a stall,
  loss of situational awareness, poor CRM. Also criticized: failure to relay a
  manufacturer bulletin warning that the MD-80 autopilot could mask a developing stall,
  inadequate high-altitude stall recognition training, broader operational deficiencies
- High-altitude stall / coffin corner analysis: heavy aircraft, climbing near ceiling,
  low excess thrust, near coffin corner — speed decayed → AOA increased → stall; pilot
  response (nose-up input) increased load factor → accelerated stall
- Link to Slide 13 model: this is the real-world consequence of not unloading first
  and not restoring stability before attempting to arrest altitude loss
- [Nok Air UPRT deck slides 45–49, citing JIAAC/BEA/NTSB findings]

## Slide 16 — FFS Practical Scope

- Practical training on manual handling at high altitude, normal and non-normal flight
  control laws/modes, emphasis on pre-stall buffet, reduced stall angle of attack vs.
  low-altitude flight, and effect of pitch inputs on trajectory/energy state
- Prompt, accurate application of the stall recovery procedure at first indication of
  an impending stall; differences between high- and low-altitude stalls addressed
- Procedures for taking over/transferring manual control; task sharing and crew
  coordination under high workload/stress, with appropriate callouts
- [FTM §2.1.13.2 Flight Path Management Table, p.2-77]
