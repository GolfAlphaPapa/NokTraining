Stage 1 · Assessment competencies

What we are assessing

PRO
Application of Procedures and Compliance with Regulations
Performs the QRH escape maneuver in the published order with the right PF and PM actions; picks reject, escape or go-around for the alert.
FPM
FPA
Flight Path Management: Manual Control and Automation
Manual: thrust, roll, pitch toward 15° within the PLI. Automatic: verifies TO/GA mode and GA thrust, monitors the system.
KNO
Application of Knowledge
Explains why we trade airspeed for altitude, and why flaps and gear stay put.
SAW
Situation Awareness and Management of Information
Spots clues and deviations early; PM monitors V/S and altitude and calls out trends.
Outcome: recognises windshear, performs the escape maneuver exactly as published (manual or automatic), and explains why.

Instructor notes

Primary competency is PRO (solid): the escape maneuver is a published procedure and the main thing assessed is that it is performed correctly and in order. FPM and FPA are the flight path competencies: FPM when the maneuver is flown manually, FPA when it is flown with automation. KNO and SAW are secondary. Read the outcome line aloud so the pilot knows what "good" looks like before any question is asked.

Five competencies are listed because the same event can be flown two ways. Optional: COM for the PM callouts of omissions and trends. Instrument scanning is tagged SAW, not FPM. Replace the observable behaviors with the exact OB wording from the current grade slip.

Expected answers and probes

Q1 (KNO). A windshear warning (two-tone siren followed by "WINDSHEAR, WINDSHEAR, WINDSHEAR") or unacceptable flight path deviations. Source: QRH Maneuvers, MAN.1.11.

Q2 (KNO). Uncontrolled changes from normal steady state flight, below 1,000 ft AGL, in excess of: 15 knots indicated airspeed, 500 fpm vertical speed, 5° pitch attitude, 1 dot displacement from the glideslope, or an unusual thrust lever position for a significant period of time. Source: QRH MAN.1.12.

Q3 (KNO). FCTM: if the pilot lowers the nose to regain airspeed, decreasing airspeed plus decreasing pitch gives a high rate of descent, and as little as 5 seconds may be available to react. In critical low-altitude situations, trade airspeed for altitude: a higher pitch attitude increases lift and improves the flight path angle, and with maximum thrust it uses the total performance of the airplane.

Q4 (PRO). Predictive warning "WINDSHEAR AHEAD, WINDSHEAR AHEAD": before V1 reject the takeoff; after V1 perform the Windshear Escape Maneuver. Probe: what if windshear is encountered (not predicted) before V1? Answer: there may not be enough runway to stop at V1; at VR rotate at a normal rate toward 15°, and once airborne perform the escape maneuver.

Q5 (PRO). Disengage autopilot; push either TO/GA switch; aggressively apply maximum thrust; disengage autothrottle; simultaneously roll wings level and rotate toward an initial pitch attitude of 15°; retract speedbrakes; follow flight director TO/GA guidance if available. Do not change flap or gear configuration. PM: verify maximum thrust; verify all needed actions are complete and call out omissions.

Q6 (FPA). Push either TO/GA switch; verify TO/GA mode annunciation; verify GA thrust; retract speedbrakes; monitor system performance. PM verifies GA thrust and calls out omissions. The manual-or-automatic items do not change: no flap or gear change, wings level unless a turn is needed for obstacles, monitor vertical speed and altitude, do not try to regain lost airspeed. Severe shear can exceed AFDS performance: be ready to disengage A/P and A/T and fly manually. Pause after each question.

Instructor notes

Matches boxes 1, 2.1 and 2.2 of the flowchart. Source: FCOM SP.16.25 to SP.16.26 (Adverse Weather, Windshear: Avoidance, Precautions). OM-A 8.3.8 repeats the avoidance text and asks crews to report windshear to ATC as soon as practicable, stating the loss or gain of speed and where it was met.

Increased VR: set V speeds for the actual gross weight, rotate at the higher rotation speed, not above actual VR + 20 kt. If shear is met at or beyond actual VR, do not try to accelerate to the increased VR; rotate without hesitation. The SOP also says assumed-temperature reduced thrust is not recommended if potential windshear exists.

Approach: "stabilized no lower than 1,000 ft above the airport" improves windshear recognition. Ask: why does a big thrust cut for a sudden airspeed increase hurt? Answer: the increase may be followed by a decrease.

Stage 3 · Theoretical knowledge

Two kinds of shear

Increasing performance

Headwind increasing, or tailwind decreasing. Airspeed rises and the airplane climbs above the planned path.

Decreasing performance

Headwind decreasing, or tailwind increasing, faster than the airplane accelerates. Airspeed falls, pitch tends down, the path drops: as little as 5 seconds to react.

A gain can be followed by a loss. No big thrust cut or trim change for a sudden airspeed increase.

Instructor notes

Read the picture left to right with the airplane. In a microburst the outflow is a headwind on the entry side (performance increases, airspeed rises), then a downdraft in the core, then a tailwind on the exit side (performance decreases, airspeed falls). The airspeed trace is a teaching sketch, not data.

CONFIRMED, FCTM chapter 7 Windshear (7.25 to 7.26): horizontal windshear may improve or degrade vertical flight path performance. Windshear that improves performance is first indicated by an increasing airspeed and may be a precursor of a shear that decreases airspeed and degrades the vertical flight path. Airspeed decreases if the tailwind increases, or the headwind decreases, faster than the airplane is accelerating; the airplane then tends to pitch down toward the in-trim speed. As little as 5 seconds may be available to react.

CONFIRMED, FCOM SP.16.26 Approach and Landing precaution: avoid large thrust reductions or trim changes in response to sudden airspeed increases as these may be followed by airspeed decreases. FCTM: the wind component is mostly horizontal below 500 ft.

DERIVED: the labels "increasing performance" and "decreasing performance" and the headwind/tailwind naming follow the FCTM wording; the downdraft is shown because the accident reports on the next slides involve microbursts. Ask: what would you do on the airspeed gain, and why?

Stage 3 · Theoretical knowledge

Why we pitch up

  1. 1Tailwind builds or headwind fades faster than the airplane accelerates
  2. 2Airspeed falls; the airplane tends to pitch down toward its in-trim speed
  3. 3Nose lowered to chase speed: descent rate builds fast
  4. 4More pitch gives more lift and a better flight path angle
  5. 5Pitch plus maximum thrust uses the full performance of the airplane
Fly the flight path. Airspeed can wait.

Instructor notes

Compare the two flight paths left to right. Both start in a headwind and cross into a tailwind, the shear that takes performance away. The picture is a teaching sketch, not to scale and not a measured model.

Source: FCTM chapter 7, Windshear, Airplane Performance in Windshear (7.25 to 7.26). Key phrases: "As little as 5 seconds may be available to recognize and react to a degrading vertical flight path" and "In critical low altitude situations, trade airspeed for altitude, if possible."

Also from the FCTM: windshear that first shows as an increasing airspeed may be a precursor of a shear that decreases airspeed. Unusual control column forces when below the in-trim speed are a cue to a possible encounter.

  1. No flap or gear change until windshear is no longer a factor

  3. Monitor vertical speed and altitude
  4. Do not try to regain lost airspeed until windshear is no longer a factor

PMPilot Monitoring

  • Monitor vertical speed and altitude
  • Call out any trend toward terrain contact, descending flight path, or significant airspeed change
WARNING Severe windshear can exceed the performance of the AFDS: be ready to disengage A/P and A/T and fly manually.

Instructor notes

Matches boxes 4.2 and "Manual or automatic flight" of the flowchart. Source: QRH MAN.1.13 to MAN.1.14. The right-hand column applies to both methods, so do not skip it when the automatic method is flown.

The QRH note on pitch: aft column force increases as airspeed decreases; the pitch attitude that gives intermittent stick shaker or initial buffet is the upper limit; flight at intermittent stick shaker may be needed to get positive terrain separation; use smooth, steady controls to avoid a pitch overshoot and a stall.

The automatic method is not a way to relax. The PM is the safety net for the monitoring items. Competency note: PRO is primary throughout. FPA is the flight path competency for the automatic column and FPM for the manual one; the corner map shows whichever is on screen.

Stage 3 · Theoretical knowledge

How high to pitch

  1. 1Rotate toward 15° together with roll to wings level
  2. 2Follow flight director TO/GA guidance, within the Pitch Limit Indication
  3. 3Aft column force rises as airspeed falls: expect it
  4. 4Intermittent shaker or initial buffet is the ceiling; stay at it only for terrain separation
  5. 5Smooth, steady inputs: no pitch overshoot
Pitch to 15°. Hold the ceiling, never break it.

Instructor notes

The scale is a sketch, not a measurement: the stick shaker pitch changes with weight, configuration and conditions, which is why the manual defines the limit by the shaker or buffet, not by a number.

Source: QRH MAN.1.13 notes (pitch limit, thrust) and FCTM chapter 7. Ask: why is overshooting the pitch worse than undershooting it a little? Probe for stall margin and for how fast the airspeed is already decaying.

Instructor notes

Keep to two or three minutes. Ask each time: which of avoid, prepare or escape would have changed this? Do not blame the crews; the reports themselves cite missing guidance, training and real-time information.

Eastern 66. NTSB-AAR-76-8, published 12 March 1976. Probable cause (summary): the airplane's encounter with adverse winds associated with a very strong thunderstorm located astride the ILS localizer course, producing a high descent rate into the non-frangible approach light towers; continued use of runway 22L was a contributing factor, since the severe weather hazard on the approach should have been evident to ATC and the flightcrew. 6 of 8 crew and 107 of 116 passengers died. Source: NTSB report abstract (TRID record 41518, trid.trb.org/View/41518).

Pan Am 759. NTSB-AAR-83-02, accident of 9 July 1982, New Orleans International (Moisant), Boeing 727-235 N4737. Probable cause: the encounter during the liftoff and initial climb with a microburst-induced wind shear which imposed a downdraft and a decreasing headwind, the effects of which the pilot would have had difficulty recognizing and reacting to in time for the descent to be arrested before impact with trees. FDR airspeed: 138 KIAS at VR, 158 KIAS at V2, 119 KIAS about 14 seconds later; maximum recorded altitude 95 ft msl. 145 aboard and 8 on the ground died; no survivors aboard. Source: libraryonline.erau.edu NTSB AAR83-02.

Delta 191. NTSB-AAR-86-05, accident of 2 August 1985, Dallas/Fort Worth, Lockheed L-1011-385-1 N726DA. Probable causes: the flightcrew's decision to initiate and continue the approach into a cumulonimbus cloud which they observed to contain visible lightning; the lack of specific guidelines, procedures and training for avoiding and escaping from low-altitude windshear; and the lack of definitive, real-time windshear hazard information; resulting in the encounter at low altitude with a microburst-induced severe windshear from a rapidly developing thunderstorm on the final approach course. 134 of 163 aboard and the driver of a car on the ground died. Source: FAA-hosted NTSB report AAR86-05 (faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/AAR86-05.pdf).

USAir 1016. NTSB-AAR-95/03, accident of 2 July 1994, Charlotte/Douglas, Douglas DC-9-31 N954VJ. Probable causes: the decision to continue an approach into severe convective activity conducive to a microburst; the failure to recognize a windshear situation in a timely manner; the failure to establish and maintain the proper airplane attitude and thrust setting necessary to escape the windshear; and the lack of real-time adverse weather and windshear hazard information from ATC. The onboard windshear detection system gave no aural or visual warning (detection thresholds were reduced because the flaps were moving). 37 of 57 aboard died. Source: libraryonline.erau.edu NTSB AAR95-03.

The one-line training points on each card are the instructor's links back to this briefing and are not NTSB findings. Pan Am 759 and Delta 191 were airplanes of other types and eras; the logic of avoid, prepare, escape is what carries over to the B737-800.

Instructor notes

Each error maps back to a line in the escape maneuver. Ask the pilot to name the step before you show the fix. The first row is the one to watch most closely: the primary competency is PRO, so a correct outcome reached by a wrong or incomplete procedure is still a finding.

Also watch for delay between the alert and the first action, and for holding the rotation too long after the aircraft is climbing.

Debrief prompts are open questions. Let the pilot talk, then tie the answer to the grid: PRO for the steps, SAW for what was noticed, KNO for the why, FPM or FPA for the flight path.
