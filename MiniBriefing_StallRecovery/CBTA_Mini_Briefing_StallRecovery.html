Stage 1 · Assessment competencies

What we are assessing

FPM
Aeroplane Flight Path Management, Manual Control
Lowers the angle of attack first, keeps wings level, and recovers the flight path with smooth, proportionate inputs.
PRO
Application of Procedures and Compliance with Regulations
Disconnects the automation and follows the QRH recovery in the correct order, without skipping or reordering steps.
KNO
Application of Knowledge
Explains why a stall is an angle of attack event and why each recovery input works.
PSD
Problem Solving and Decision Making
Recognises the cue, commits to recovery without hesitation, and adapts to the scenario and altitude margin.
Outcome: the pilot recovers from each stall scenario in the correct sequence and explains why.

Expected answers and probes

Q1 (KNO). Exceeding the critical angle of attack. Airflow separates from the upper surface and lift falls. Not airspeed alone, and not pitch attitude. Probe: what is the difference between pitch attitude and angle of attack?

Q2 (KNO). Yes. The wing stalls whenever the critical angle of attack is reached, at any airspeed, attitude or altitude, for example in a steep pull-up or a steep turn. Probe: can you give an example where speed is high and you still stall?

Q3 (KNO). Load factor increases the lift required. At a given speed more lift means more angle of attack, so the critical angle is reached at a higher speed. Stall speed rises by the square root of the load factor: about +19% at 45° bank, +41% at 60°.

Q4 (PRO, FPM). Reduce the angle of attack: disconnect the automation and apply nose-down elevator until the stall warning stops. Probe: why not add thrust only? VERIFY the exact first items against the QRH approach-to-stall and stall recovery checklists.

Q5 (PSD, KNO). Same principle in all three: reduce the angle of attack first. What changes is altitude margin, drag, thrust demand and startle risk. After takeoff has the smallest margin.

Pause after each question. Do not advance until the pilot has committed to an answer.

Instructor notes

Ask first: "What happens to lift if I keep increasing angle of attack?" Let the pilot answer, then drag the slider. Use the presets or the slider (Up and Down keys change the angle by 2° on this slide).

Point at the peak: that is the critical angle of attack. Beyond it lift falls even though angle of attack is still increasing. The dashed line before the peak is the stall warning, which activates before the critical angle. This curve is a teaching shape, not a measured 737 curve.

Link to Q1 and Q2: the critical angle is about the wing, not the speed. Reveal the chain one line at a time and ask "and then what?" before each step. Step 5 sets up the recovery: nothing else fixes a stall except getting the angle of attack back below critical.

Instructor notes

Ask first: "Does the aircraft always stall at the same speed?" Let them answer, then show the bars. Stall speed multiplier is the square root of the load factor: 1.00, 1.07, 1.19, 1.41 at 0°, 30°, 45°, 60° bank in a level turn.

Make the point visually: the wing did not change, the load on it did. Link back to Q2 and Q3, then reveal the three lines and ask for an example of each.

The flap statement is a teaching simplification from general knowledge: flaps lower the stall speed but the critical angle of attack is lower with flaps extended. Check the wording against the FCTM or FCOM before quoting it.

Stage 3 · Three scenarios

Same principle, different margins

Clean

  • CONFIGFlaps and gear up
  • ENERGYHigher speed and altitude, thrust builds slowly
  • MARGINMost altitude to recover
  • HAZARDOver-pushing into overspeed, or pulling too early

Full configuration

  • CONFIGLanding flaps, gear down
  • ENERGYHigh drag, low speed, thrust needed early
  • MARGINLow altitude on approach
  • HAZARDLosing altitude close to the ground

After takeoff

  • CONFIGTakeoff flaps, gear retracting or up
  • ENERGYHigh thrust, strong pitch-up tendency
  • MARGINSmallest altitude margin
  • HAZARDStartle, and pulling to keep climbing
Different margins. Same first move: reduce the angle of attack.

Instructor notes

Ask first: "Which of these three gives you the least room for error, and why?" Let the pilot answer, then reveal the columns one at a time. Expected: after takeoff, because of low altitude and the high thrust pitch-up tendency.

The point of the slide is the last bar: the margins change, the principle does not. This links back to Q5.

Column details are teaching summaries, not Boeing wording. Verify configuration definitions (flap settings, gear state, thrust) in the FTM scenario descriptions before presenting.

Stage 4 · Scenario 1 · Clean

Flaps up, gear up

ASK FIRST

Clean, level flight, speed decaying. The stick shaker activates. What do you do first, and why?

THEN REVEAL
  1. Recognise the cue and announce it
  2. Disconnect autopilot and autothrottle
  3. Nose-down elevator until the stall warning stops
  4. Wings level, thrust as needed, speedbrakes retracted
  5. Return to the flight path smoothly, no secondary stall
Watch for: pulling too early, or pushing into overspeed. Most altitude to recover, so lower the angle first.Action summary for the draft. Use the actual QRH wording in the final briefing. VERIFY

Instructor notes

Ask the question and wait. Expected: reduce the angle of attack, because only a lower angle of attack restores lift. Then reveal the recovery steps one at a time and ask the pilot to name the step before each appears.

Clean has the most altitude to recover, so there is no reason to rush the pull-out. Probe: what happens if you pull back as soon as the shaker stops?

Sequence and wording follow the general approach-to-stall template. Confirm the QRH items, order and thrust instruction before presenting. VERIFY

Stage 4 · Scenario 2 · Full configuration

Landing flaps, gear down

ASK FIRST

On approach, landing flaps and gear down, low altitude. The shaker activates. How far do you lower the nose, and how long?

THEN REVEAL
  1. Recognise the cue and announce it
  2. Disconnect autopilot and autothrottle
  3. Nose-down only until the stall warning stops
  4. Wings level and thrust as needed, early
  5. Stabilise the flight path, then decide on the approach
Watch for: over-pushing and losing altitude close to the ground. Reduce the angle only as much as needed.Configuration changes during recovery per QRH and FCTM. VERIFY

Instructor notes

Ask the question and wait. Expected: only until the warning stops. A bigger push than needed wastes altitude you do not have, but not pushing enough keeps the wing stalled.

High drag with gear and flaps out means thrust is needed earlier. Probe: how does a decision to continue or go around fit after the recovery? That is the PSD link.

Do not state flap or gear actions during the recovery from memory. Confirm in the QRH and FCTM before presenting. VERIFY

Stage 4 · Scenario 3 · After takeoff

Takeoff flaps, gear up

ASK FIRST

Just after takeoff, high thrust, low altitude, shaker. Why is this the hardest recovery to do well?

THEN REVEAL
  1. Recognise the cue and announce it
  2. Disconnect automation if engaged
  3. Nose-down elevator until the stall warning stops
  4. Wings level, thrust as the QRH directs
  5. Stabilise the climb; PM calls altitude and speed
Watch for: startle, then pulling to keep climbing. Smallest margin, so reduce the angle first, then stabilise.Engine failure variations and thrust instruction per QRH and FTM. VERIFY

Instructor notes

Ask the question and wait. Expected: lowest altitude margin, high thrust gives a strong pitch-up tendency, and the startle response is to pull. All three make the correct input feel wrong.

Debrief the first reaction before the technique. Probe: what did your hands do before your head caught up?

If the scenario includes an engine failure, add the asymmetric thrust and rudder considerations from the FTM and QRH, not from this draft. VERIFY

Debrief prompts

  1. What did you notice first, and what was your first reaction?
  2. Why did lowering the angle of attack work?

Instructor notes

Rows 1 to 5 are typical errors and each maps back to the recovery steps. Ask the pilot to name the step that was missed before you show the fix. Row 1 is the one to stress: the instinct is to protect altitude by pulling, and that keeps the wing stalled.

Debrief prompts are open questions. Let the pilot talk and then tie the answer to the competency grid: SAW for what was noticed, KNO for the why, FPM for the control inputs.
