Expected answers and probes

Q1 (KNO). More than 25° nose up, more than 10° nose down, or bank more than 45°. An upset can also be within those values but at an airspeed inappropriate for the conditions. Probe: where is this definition written? (ICAO Doc 10011, EASA UPRT definitions, FAA AC 120-111.)

Q2 (KNO). It reduces angle of attack, which makes the roll controls effective again and gives a nose-down pitch rate. Probe: why does it feel wrong, and what does your body want to do instead?

Q3 (SAW). Startle is the involuntary physical and cognitive reaction. Surprise is the mismatch between what you expected and what you see. Both can produce a delayed or wrong first input. Countermeasure: recognise, confirm, announce, then follow the template.

Q4 (FPM). Roll in the shortest direction to wings level, then nose-up elevator and thrust or drag as needed. Do not pull first. VERIFY the unload wording for very high bank against the FCTM.

Q5 (FPM). Nose-down control input to get a nose-down pitch rate. Reduce thrust if underwing engines add to the pitch-up. Use roll adjustment (avoid more than 60° bank) only if needed.

Pause after each question. Do not advance until the pilot has committed to an answer.

Stage 3 · Theoretical knowledge

What is an upset?

  • > 25°Pitch attitude, nose up
  • > 10°Pitch attitude, nose down
  • > 45°Bank angle
  • ORWithin these limits, but at an airspeed inappropriate for the conditions
Startle
Involuntary physical and cognitive reaction
Surprise
What you see is not what you expected
Recognise. Confirm. Announce.

Instructor notes

Use the presets or the sliders (Up and Down keys change pitch by 5° on this slide). Show that one parameter outside the normal zone is enough to call it an upset: the status text changes to UPSET and names the cause.

Definition: ICAO Doc 10011 and EASA UPRT definitions (also FAA AC 120-111): pitch more than 25° nose up, more than 10° nose down, bank more than 45°, or within those but at an inappropriate airspeed.

Startle and surprise are defined separately in the same material. Both can delay or distort the first response, which is why recognition and announcement come first in every template.

Stage 3 · Theoretical knowledge

Why the inputs work

  1. 1Nose high: speed decays and AOA climbs
  2. 2High AOA: flow separates, roll controls weaken
  3. 3Nose-down elevator lowers AOA
  4. 4Flow reattaches: roll controls work again
  5. 5Now roll can help bring the nose down
Nose high: lower the AOA first. Nose low: roll the lift upright first.

Instructor notes

Compare the two wing sections left to right. In the right-hand picture the upper-surface flow breaks away and the aileron is drawn dashed to show reduced effect. This is a teaching simplification, not a measured aerodynamic model.

The industry recovery material explains that continuous nose-down elevator keeps the wing AOA as low as possible, which makes the normal roll controls effective. Ask the pilot: "Why does pushing feel wrong when the nose is high?"

Nose low is different: the lift vector is pointed sideways, so the first priority is to roll it upright, then pull smoothly. Do not pull before the wings are level.

Stage 3 · Recovery templates

Two upsets, two sequences

Nose high PUSH FIRST

  1. Recognise, confirm, announce "NOSE HIGH"
  2. Disconnect A/P and A/T
  3. Nose-down elevator, up to full; trim nose-down as needed
  4. Reduce thrust if underwing engines add pitch-up
  5. Roll to get a nose-down rate (avoid more than 60° bank)
  6. Near horizon: wings level, check speed and thrust, set pitch

Nose low ROLL FIRST

  1. Recognise, confirm, announce "NOSE LOW"
  2. Disconnect A/P and A/T
  3. Recover from stall first, if required
  4. Roll the shortest way to wings level VERIFY UNLOAD
  5. Nose-up elevator; nose-up trim if required
  6. Adjust thrust and drag (speedbrake) as required
Nose high: push, then roll. Nose low: roll, then pull. Call attitude, airspeed and altitude throughout. Excess pitch trim or rudder can make the upset worse.

Instructor notes

Both columns reveal in step so the pilot sees the same step number side by side. Ask "which step is different first?" Answer: step 3.

Sequence follows the industry upset recovery training aid and the OEM templates published in EASA GM3 to ORO.FC.220 and 230, and the Boeing FCTM maneuvers guidance. Confirm the exact FCTM wording and any bank threshold for unloading in very high bank before presenting. VERIFY

The "avoid more than 60° bank" limit applies to the roll adjustment used to get a nose-down pitch rate in a nose-high upset.

Debrief prompts

Instructor notes

Rows 1 to 4 are typical errors and each maps back to a step in the templates. Ask the pilot to name the step before you show the fix. Also watch for premature recovery causing a secondary stall, and for ignoring overspeed in a nose-low recovery.

Debrief prompts are open questions. Let the pilot talk and then tie the answer to the competency grid: SAW for what was noticed, KNO for the why, FPM for the control inputs.
